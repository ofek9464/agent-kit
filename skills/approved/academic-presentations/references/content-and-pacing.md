# Content and pacing

Use for the slide sequence, academic framing, and speaking-time estimate. Follow the assignment and actual evidence; the patterns below are options, not a mandatory chapter template.

## Project defense

Build the sequence around what the examiners need to assess: the problem and scope, relevant background, what the authors designed or investigated, why consequential choices were made, what evidence was obtained, and what the results support. Include constraints, limitations, and the authors' specific contribution where they change the assessment.

Do not convert a report chapter by chapter into slides. Select the reasoning and evidence needed to understand the work within the available time. Use implementation detail when it explains a decision, mechanism, or limitation. Name contributions accurately; do not attribute a library's functionality or someone else's work to the project team.

Results slides should identify the comparison, units, test setup needed for interpretation, and the supported finding. Preserve meaningful failures or discrepancies. A plausible illustrative chart is not a measured result. Missing results can be identified as pending or omitted from a conclusion, never supplied by invention.

## Coursework and teaching

Use the learning objective to choose the sequence. Introduce prerequisites only when the audience needs them, explain the mechanism or concept, and use a relevant example or comparison. Do not force every class presentation into a problem-method-results defense structure.

## Estimate presentation time

Record total allocated time and whether questions, demonstration, or other activities are included. Subtract included activities and allow for transitions to find the time available for slides. If those allocations are unknown and materially change the plan, ask; otherwise identify the estimate as an assumption.

Assign approximate speaking time to each planned slide based on the material: a brief orientation needs less explanation than a dense diagram or a result requiring setup. Sum the estimates and compare with the available slide time. This is a planning estimate, not proof of rehearsal timing.

If the sequence is too long, remove repeated context, combine low-value transitions, select the decisive evidence, or move optional detail to an agreed backup section. If essential material still does not fit, present the conflict and resolve scope or duration with the user. Avoid squeezing the same script into smaller text or assuming the speaker can simply talk faster.

If the plan is too short, deepen useful explanation or examples when the objective warrants it. Do not pad the deck to hit an arbitrary count. An explicit slide count remains a constraint; covers and backups count unless the user says otherwise.

## Text and spoken explanation

For sparse slides, retain the terms, labels, units, and qualifications needed to interpret visuals. Put the spoken reasoning in notes when requested. For self-contained slides, use concise explanatory text with readable grouping; do not assume a dense paragraph is necessary for completeness.

Use short subject titles for setup and definitions, and supported takeaway titles for findings. Avoid a mandatory claim-shaped title on every slide. Read the titles in sequence, then inspect whether each slide's body and evidence serve that sequence without repetition.
